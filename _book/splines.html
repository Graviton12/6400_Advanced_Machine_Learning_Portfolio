6  Splines

6.1 Motivating Idea of Splines

6.1.1 The Linear Model:

Suppose we measure two quantities, such as a child’s age (\(x\)) and child’s length (\(y\)) on many children. In regression (prediction modeling), we have a function \(f\) which takes in an input \(x\) and returns an average output at that input. The goal of prediction modeling is to learn \(f\) from data, so that for a new child we can predict their length using \(f(x)\).

Children of the same age do not all have the same length. We therefore write the observed length as the average length at that age \(f(x)\) plus the individual’s random deviation from that average, called the noise, \(\varepsilon\):

\[ \underbrace{y}_{\text{observed length of a child at age x}} = \underbrace{f(x)}_{\text{true average length of child at age x (unknown)}} + \underbrace{\varepsilon}_{\text{noise}} \] We never observe \(f\) directly, so we estimate it from data and write the estimate as \(\hat f\). The predicted length of a child at age \(x\) is given by:

\[ \underbrace{\hat y}_{\text{predicted length at age } x} = \underbrace{\hat f(x)}_{\text{estimate of the average length at age } x} \]

The gap between what we observed and what we predicted is the residual. It is our estimate of the noise \(\varepsilon\):

\[ \underbrace{\hat\varepsilon}_{\text{residual}} = \underbrace{y}_{\text{observed}} - \underbrace{\hat y}_{\text{predicted}} \]

The true average \(f(x)\) is called the conditional mean. It is the expected (average) value of the outcome \(Y\), given the predictor value \(x\):

\[ E[Y \mid x] = f(x) \]

The simplest choice of \(f\) is a straight line:

\[ f(x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x \]

With With \(p\) number of predictors \(x_1,\dots,x_p\) collected in a vector \(\mathbf{x}\), the straight line becomes a weighted sum:

\[ E[Y \mid \mathbf{x}] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p = \sum_{j=0}^{p} x_j\beta_j = \mathbf{x}\boldsymbol\beta \]

The numbers \(\beta_j\) are the model’s parameters (coefficients), and fitting the model means choosing them using the data.

  • linear in \(\boldsymbol\beta\) (the parameters) means each \(\beta\) is scaled by something or added to something (linear cominbation). No \(\beta\) is squared, put in an exponent, or multiplied by another \(\beta\).
  • linear in \(x\) (the predictor) means the curve is a straight line when you plot it.
Model Linear in \(x\)? Linear in \(\boldsymbol\beta\)?
\(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x^2\) no yes
\(\beta_0 + \beta_1^2 x\) yes no
\(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x\) yes yes

The model also says how spread out the noise is around the average which is the conditional variance. We assume the spread is the same everywhere:

\[ \text{Var}(Y \mid \mathbf{x}) = \sigma^2 . \]

For a single child \(i\), the model can be written out in full. This is called the scalar form:

\[ y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \cdots + \beta_p x_{ip} + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Normal}(0,\ \sigma^2). \] With \(n\) children we stack everything into vectors which can be represented as matrices:

\[ Y = X\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon, \qquad \boldsymbol\varepsilon \sim \text{Normal}(\mathbf{0}_n,\ \sigma^2 I_n). \]

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ \vdots \\ y_n \end{bmatrix}}_{Y\ (n\times 1)} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_{11} & x_{12} & \cdots & x_{1p} \\ 1 & x_{21} & x_{22} & \cdots & x_{2p} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 1 & x_{n1} & x_{n2} & \cdots & x_{np} \end{bmatrix}}_{X\ (n\times (p+1))} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \beta_0 \\ \beta_1 \\ \vdots \\ \beta_p \end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol\beta\ ((p+1)\times 1)} + \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \varepsilon_1 \\ \varepsilon_2 \\ \vdots \\ \varepsilon_n \end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol\varepsilon\ (n\times 1)} \]

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \varepsilon_1 \\ \varepsilon_2 \\ \vdots \\ \varepsilon_n \end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol\varepsilon} \sim \text{Normal}\left( \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{0}_n},\ \sigma^2 \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{I_n\ (n\times n)} \right) \]

NoteNotation
  • \(Y\): \(n \times 1\) outcome vector; \(Y_i\) is the outcome for each observation \(i\).
  • \(\mathbf{x}\): the predictor vector of predictor values for each observation \(i\) (with a leading 1 for the intercept). A plain \(x\) is a single predictor.
  • \(X\): \(n \times (p+1)\) design matrix; the first column is all ones for the intercept; \(X_{ij}\) is the \(j\)-th predictor value for observation \(i\).
  • \(\beta_j\): are the model’s parameters (coefficients). Fitting the model means choosing them using the data.
  • \(\boldsymbol\beta\): \((p+1) \times 1\) coefficient vector; \(\beta_0\) is the intercept and \(\beta_j\) is the coefficient for the \(j\)-th predictor.
  • \(\mathbf{x}\boldsymbol\beta\): the dot product of the predictor vector with the coefficient vector (using matrix multiplication).
  • \(\sigma^2\): scalar, the conditional variance (how spread out the noise is around the average).
  • \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\): \(n \times 1\) error vector; one noise term each observation \(i\).
  • \(\mathbf{0}_n\): vector of all zeros of length \(n\).
  • \(I_n\): identity matrix of size \(n\) (ones on the diagonal, zeros elsewhere).

6.1.2 What Is a Spline, and Why Do We Need One:

The problem: a straight line is too rigid. The model \(f(x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\) says that every extra month of a child’s age adds the same \(\beta_1\) cm of length, at every age. Real growth however, plateaus where the child may grow more rapidaly during the early motnhs of infancy but for later months of infancy, they grow as a slwoer rate, creating a bending in the curve. A straight line cannot bend, so it will over-predict in some regions and under-predict in others, no matter how much data we collect. (A multiple linear regression is not a good fit).

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 150
age = rng.uniform(0, 36, n)

def f_true(a):
    return 50 + 12.8 * np.log1p(a)          # true average length (fast early, slower later)

length_cm = f_true(age) + rng.normal(0, 2.5, n)   # observed = true average + noise

# straight-line (least squares) fit and its residuals
slope, intercept = np.polyfit(age, length_cm, 1)
resid = length_cm - (intercept + slope * age)
age_grid = np.linspace(0, 36, 200)

# average residual within age bins (a simple smooth)
edges = np.linspace(0, 36, 9)
centers = (edges[:-1] + edges[1:]) / 2
bin_means = [resid[(age >= lo) & (age < hi)].mean()
             for lo, hi in zip(edges[:-1], edges[1:])]

fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 3.8))

ax[0].scatter(age, length_cm, s=14, color="steelblue", alpha=0.6)
ax[0].plot(age_grid, intercept + slope * age_grid, color="darkorange", lw=3,
           label="Straight line")
ax[0].plot(age_grid, f_true(age_grid), "r--", lw=2, label="True average $f(x)$")
ax[0].set(xlabel="Age (months)", ylabel="Length (cm)", title="Straight-line fit")
ax[0].legend(loc="lower right", frameon=False)

ax[1].scatter(age, resid, s=14, color="steelblue", alpha=0.6)
ax[1].axhline(0, color="black", ls="--")
ax[1].plot(centers, bin_means, color="purple", lw=3, marker="o",
           label="Average residual per age bin")
ax[1].set(xlabel="Age (months)", ylabel=r"Residual $\hat{\varepsilon}$",
          title="Residuals of the straight line")
ax[1].legend(loc="lower center", frameon=False)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

The straight line sits above the data at the youngest and oldest ages, so it over-predicts there (negative residuals). In the middle it sits below the data, so it under-predicts (positive residuals). If the line were a good model, the residuals would scatter randomly around zero. Instead the purple curve shows a systematic upside-down U pattern. This is the signal that the true relationship bends, and collecting more data will not fix it.

6.2 Basis Expansion

The key idea: The linear spline is a linear model, meaning it is linear in the parameters \(\boldsymbol\beta\) even though the relationship between the child’s length and age is not a straight line, so it is not linear in \(x\). The fitting method from the previous section only needs the model to be a weighted sum with the \(\beta\)’s as weights. It does not care what the quantities being weighted are. So instead of feeding the model \(x\) itself, we can feed it features of \(x\), called basis functions \(b_1(x), \dots, b_M(x)\). This is called a basis expansion:

\[ E[Y \mid x] = \beta_0 + \sum_{m=0}^{M} \beta_m\, b_m(x) \]

\[ E[Y \mid x] = \sum_{m=0}^{M} \beta_m\, b_m(x), \qquad b_0(x) = 1. \]

A basis function allows us to expand the predictor \(x\) into a vector of features \(\Phi(x) = \big(b_0(x), b_1(x), \dots, b_M(x)\big)'\) and build the curve as a weighted sum of them. Each basis function evaluated at the data becomes one column of the design matrix, with entries \(X_{im} = b_m(x_i)\). Everything else is unchanged:

\[ Y = X\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon \quad\longrightarrow\quad Y = \Phi(X)\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon, \qquad \]

This works like building the point \((3,2)\) from the blocks \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\) as \(3(1,0) + 2(0,1)\). The coefficients \(\beta_m\) say how much of each block to use. For example, the basis \(1, x, x^2\) builds every curve of the form \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2\). Each basis function evaluated at the data becomes one column of the design matrix \(X\).

Replacing \(x\) with basis functions \(b_0(x), \dots, b_M(x)\) changes only the columns of the design matrix. We write the expanded design matrix as \(\Phi\):

\[ \underbrace{X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_1 \\ 1 & x_2 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_n \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{raw design matrix } (n\times 2)} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \underbrace{\Phi(X) = \begin{bmatrix} b_0(x_1) & b_1(x_1) & \cdots & b_M(x_1) \\ b_0(x_2) & b_1(x_2) & \cdots & b_M(x_2) \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ b_0(x_n) & b_1(x_n) & \cdots & b_M(x_n) \end{bmatrix}}_{\Phi\ (n\times (M+1))} \]

The entry in row \(i\) and column \(m\) is \(\Phi_{im} = b_m(x_i)\): basis function \(m\) evaluated at observation \(i\). The model keeps the same form:

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ \vdots \\ y_n \end{bmatrix}}_{Y\ (n\times 1)} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} b_0(x_1) & b_1(x_1) & \cdots & b_M(x_1) \\ b_0(x_2) & b_1(x_2) & \cdots & b_M(x_2) \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ b_0(x_n) & b_1(x_n) & \cdots & b_M(x_n) \end{bmatrix}}_{\Phi\ (n\times (M+1))} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \beta_0 \\ \beta_1 \\ \vdots \\ \beta_M \end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol\beta\ ((M+1)\times 1)} + \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \varepsilon_1 \\ \varepsilon_2 \\ \vdots \\ \varepsilon_n \end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol\varepsilon\ (n\times 1)} \]

that is, \(Y = \Phi\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon\). Row \(i\) multiplies out to

\[ y_i = \beta_0 b_0(x_i) + \beta_1 b_1(x_i) + \cdots + \beta_M b_M(x_i) + \varepsilon_i, \]

which is the weighted sum of basis functions plus noise. The least squares estimate has the same form as before, with \(\Phi\) in place of \(X\):

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\Phi'\Phi)^{-1}\Phi'Y . \]

Dimension check: \(\Phi'\) is \((M+1)\times n\), so \(\Phi'\Phi\) is \((M+1)\times(M+1)\), \(\Phi'Y\) is \((M+1)\times 1\), and therefore \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) is \((M+1)\times 1\), one coefficient per basis function.

Example. The three children with ages \(2, 8, 18\) onths respectively and the parabola basis \(b_0(x) = 1,\ b_1(x) = x,\ b_2(x) = x^2\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ y_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 8 & 64 \\ 1 & 18 & 324 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \beta_0 \\ \beta_1 \\ \beta_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} \varepsilon_1 \\ \varepsilon_2 \\ \varepsilon_3 \end{bmatrix} \]

Each piece only has to describe the data nearby, so the curve is flexible where it needs to be, local, and stable, and because it is still a basis expansion, it is still a linear in \(\boldsymbol\beta\) even though the curve is a parabola in \(x\).

Model Columns of \(X\) Shape in \(x\)
Straight line \(1,\ x\) line
Parabola \(1,\ x,\ x^2\) parabola
Spline \(1,\ x,\ \dots\) (built from knots) flexible curve

6.2.1 Types of Basis Functions:

One type of basis expansions are Polynomials:

Take \(b_d(x) = x^d\), so that

\[ x \longrightarrow \{1,\ x,\ x^2,\ x^3,\ \dots,\ x^p\}, \qquad d = 0, 1, 2, \dots, p. \]

  1. Pros: smooth curve (no discontinuities)
  2. Cons: It is a global function: every coefficient affects the whole curve (function), so changing one coefficient \(\beta_m\) changes \(f(x)\) everywhere, and data at one end of the range can change the fit at the other end. High-degree polynomials also tend to oscillate between data points and to diverge in the tails.

Another type of basis expansions are Piecewise Polynomials:

  • 0th degree - piecewise constant
  • 1st degree - piecewise linear
  • 2nd degree - piecewise quadratic
  • 3rd degree - piecewise cubic
  • etc.
  1. Pros: It is a local function: local chnages only affect the segemnt they occur in rather than the entire curve (function), so changing one coefficient \(\beta\) will chnage change the specifc segment of the curve.
  2. Cons: Nothing forces neighboring peices to meet so there can be discontinuities and jumps in the curve where the knots begin and end. We must choose where these knots begin and end.

Another type of basis expansions are Spline:

A compromise of botb Degree Polynomials and Piecewise Polynomials:

  1. Pro: smooth curve (no discontinuities) and a local function since changes in coefficient \(\beta\) will chnage change the specifc segment of the curve defined by the knots which are specific pointd which breaks up the x-axis into segments.

A spline is built in three steps:

  1. Cut the \(x\)-axis into segments at chosen points called knots. The knots control where the slope of the curve is allowed to change.
  2. Fit a simple, low-degree polynomial on each piece.
  3. Stitch the pieces together so the joins of the curves are smooth.

6.2.2 Piecewise Polynomials and Stitched Splines

Splines act as stitched piecewise polynomials when fit to the data. The figure below fits the same noisy data in two ways. The columns are the degrees \(d = 0, 1, 2, 3\), and the knots (dashed lines) are at \(x = 0.25, 0.5, 0.75\).

Code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 80
x = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 1, n))
y = np.sin(2 * np.pi * x) + rng.normal(0, 0.2, n)

knots = np.array([0.25, 0.5, 0.75])
edges = np.concatenate([[0], knots, [1]])
K = len(knots)
names = {0: "constant", 1: "linear", 2: "quadratic", 3: "cubic"}

def trunc_basis(x, d):
    cols = [x**j for j in range(d + 1)]
    cols += [np.where(x > k, (x - k)**d, 0.0) for k in knots]
    return np.column_stack(cols)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 4, figsize=(13, 6), sharex=True, sharey=True)
for d in range(4):
    # Top row: a separate polynomial on each piece
    ax = axes[0, d]
    ax.scatter(x, y, s=10, color="steelblue", alpha=0.5)
    for lo, hi in zip(edges[:-1], edges[1:]):
        m = (x >= lo) & (x < hi) if hi < 1 else (x >= lo) & (x <= hi)
        coef = np.polyfit(x[m], y[m], d)
        g = np.linspace(lo, hi, 60)
        ax.plot(g, np.polyval(coef, g), color="darkorange", lw=2.5)
    for k in knots:
        ax.axvline(k, color="gray", ls="--", lw=1)
    ax.set_title(f"d = {d} ({names[d]})\n{(K+1)*(d+1)} parameters", fontsize=10)

    # Bottom row: the spline (truncated power basis, least squares)
    ax = axes[1, d]
    ax.scatter(x, y, s=10, color="steelblue", alpha=0.5)
    beta = np.linalg.lstsq(trunc_basis(x, d), y, rcond=None)[0]
    g = np.linspace(0, 1, 400)
    ax.plot(g, trunc_basis(g, d) @ beta, color="purple", lw=2.5)
    for k in knots:
        ax.axvline(k, color="gray", ls="--", lw=1)
    ax.set_title(f"{K+d+1} parameters", fontsize=10)
    ax.set_xlabel("x")

axes[0, 0].set_ylabel("Separate fit on each piece\ny")
axes[1, 0].set_ylabel("Stitched: spline\ny")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

In the top row, each piece is fit on its own, so nothing forces neighboring pieces to meet: the orange curves jump at the knots, and the number of parameters grows quickly as \((K+1)(d+1)\). In the bottom row we use the same degree and the same knots, but we require the curve and its first \(d-1\) derivatives to match at every knot. The result is connected (and smooth for \(d \ge 2\)) and needs only \(K+d+1\) parameters. For \(d = 0\) there is nothing to match, so both rows show the same step function. The rest of this chapter makes the bottom row precise.

6.3 General Definition of Splines

Splines address polynomial instability by fitting piecewise polynomials constrained to join smoothly at selected values of called knots. Each piece is a low-degree polynomial on its own interval; the constraints at the knots enforce continuity and smoothness.

NoteDefinition

A spline with \(K\) knots \(\kappa_1, \dots, \kappa_K\) is a function \(f(x)\) such that

  1. on each of the \(K+1\) intervals, \(f(\kappa)\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(d=1\), and
  2. \(f(\kappa)\) and its derivatives \(f'(\kappa), f''(\kappa), \dots, f^{(d-1)}(\kappa)\) are continuous at every knot \(K\).

The first derivative \(f'(\kappa)\) is the slope of the curve, where the curve contians kinks (non-differentiable corners) at the knots, and the second derivative \(f''(\kappa)\) is how fast the slope is changing (the curvature).

Degree \(d\) Name Continuous at the knots
0 piecewise constant (step function) nothing required
1 linear spline \(f(\kappa)\) (contains kinks)
2 quadratic spline \(f(\kappa)\), \(f'(\kappa)\)
3 cubic spline \(f(\kappa)\), \(f'(\kappa)\), \(f''(\kappa)\)

6.4 Linear Splines (Piecewise Linear)

NoteDefinition

A linear spline is the spline of degree \(d = 1\): a function made of straight-line pieces that are joined end to end at the knots. Between knots the curve is a straight line. At each knot the line is allowed to change slope, creating a “sharp corner” or “kink”. The curve is continuous everywhere else.

For child growth, this lets the curve rise steeply in early infancy and then bend to a gentler slope later, without jumping.

6.4.1 Mathematical Derivation:

Let \(\kappa_1, \dots, \kappa_K\) be the \(K\) knots. The knots cut into \(K+1\) intervals

\[ I_j = (\kappa_{j-1},\ \kappa_j], \qquad j = 1, \dots, K+1 . \]

The whole spline is \(f(x)\). On each interval it follows its own straight line \(f_j(x)\):

\[ f_j(x) = \alpha_j + \gamma_j\, x, \qquad f(x) = f_j(x) \ \text{ for } x \in I_j , \]

where \(\alpha_j\) is the intercept and \(\gamma_j\) is the slope of the line used on interval \(I_j\). Each \(f_j(x)\) is a full straight line that could be drawn for any \(x\), but it equals the spline \(f(x)\) only on \(I_j\).

6.4.2 Step 1: Impose continuity at the knots:

For \(d = 1\), condition 2 of the definition requires only that \(f(x)\) itself be continuous at every knot. At knot \(\kappa_j\), the line from the left side fo the line \(f_j(\kappa_j)\) and the line from the right side of the line \(f_{j+1}(\kappa_j)\) must have the same value knot \(\kappa_j\):

\[ f_j(\kappa_j) = f_{j+1}(\kappa_j) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \alpha_j + \gamma_j\,\kappa_j = \alpha_{j+1} + \gamma_{j+1}\,\kappa_j, \qquad j = 1, \dots, K . \]

6.4.3 Step 2: Determine how the slopes betweeen segments differ:

At a knot, the two lines segments meet. Step 1 told us they must be at the same height at the knot. In step 2, we ar enow interested in seeing how the slopes differ between two line segments. To do this, we first take the difference between the right line segment and the left line segment. This difference is defined by \(\delta_j(x)\):

\[ \delta_j(x) = f_{j+1}(x) - f_j(x) = (\alpha_{j+1} - \alpha_j) + (\gamma_{j+1} - \gamma_j)\,x . \]

\(\delta_j(x)\) is itself a straight line. Continuity at \(\kappa_j\) says \(f_j(\kappa_j) = f_{j+1}(\kappa_j)\), so

\[ \delta_j(\kappa_j) = 0 . \]

Subtracting \(\delta_j(\kappa_j) = 0\) from \(\delta_j(x)\) cancels the constant term:

\[ \delta_j(x) = \delta_j(x) - \delta_j(\kappa_j) = (\gamma_{j+1} - \gamma_j)\,(x - \kappa_j). \]

A line that is zero at \(\kappa_j\) must be a multiple of \((x - \kappa_j)\), and the multiple is the change in slope. Give it a name:

\[ \beta_{j+1} = \gamma_{j+1} - \gamma_j . \]

Therefore each line is the previous line plus a slope change that starts at the knot:

\[ \boxed{\,f_{j+1}(x) = f_j(x) + \beta_{j+1}(x - \kappa_j)\,} \]

6.4.4 Step 3: Applyig step 2 for all knot values (for each interval):

Start with the first interval and name its intercept and slope \(\beta_0 = \alpha_1\) and \(\beta_1 = \gamma_1\), so that

\[ f_1(x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x . \]

Applying Step 2 repeatedly:

\[ \begin{aligned} f_2(x) &= f_1(x) + \beta_2(x - \kappa_1) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2(x - \kappa_1), \\ f_3(x) &= f_2(x) + \beta_3(x - \kappa_2) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2(x - \kappa_1) + \beta_3(x - \kappa_2), \\ &\ \ \vdots \\ f_j(x) &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \sum_{k=1}^{j-1} \beta_{k+1}\,(x - \kappa_k). \end{aligned} \]

Since \(f(x) = f_j(x)\) for \(x \in I_j\), we have

\[ f(x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \sum_{k=1}^{j-1} \beta_{k+1}\,(x - \kappa_k), \qquad x \in I_j . \]

6.4.5 Step 4: Write one formula for all \(x\):

The formula in Step 3 includes a different number of terms on each interval. We can write one formula by using the positive part

\[ (u)^+ = \max(0,\, u) = \begin{cases} u, & u > 0 \\ 0, & u \le 0 . \end{cases} \]

Take \(x \in I_j = (\kappa_{j-1}, \kappa_j]\) and check that \((x - \kappa_k)^+\) switches each term on or off correctly:

  • For knots already passed (\(k \le j-1\)): \(x > \kappa_{j-1} \ge \kappa_k\), so \((x - \kappa_k)^+ = x - \kappa_k\). The term is on, as in Step 3.
  • For knots not yet reached (\(k \ge j\)): \(x \le \kappa_j \le \kappa_k\), so \((x - \kappa_k)^+ = 0\). The term is off.

Because \(f(x) = f_j(x)\) on \(I_j\), a single formula for \(f(x)\) is correct on every interval at once:

\[ \boxed{\,f(x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \beta_{k+1}\,(x - \kappa_k)^+\,} \]

The model for the data is therefore

\[ E[Y \mid x] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \beta_{k+1}\,(x - \kappa_k)^+, \qquad \text{Var}(Y \mid x) = \sigma^2 . \]

For example, with two knots,

\[ E[Y \mid x] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 (x - \kappa_1)^+ + \beta_3 (x - \kappa_2)^+ . \]

6.4.6 Step 5: Read off the basis functions:

Label each term in the boxed formula from Step 4 by the basis functions \(b_0(x)=1, b_1(x)=x, b_2(x)=x^2, \cdots\) that multiplies its coefficient:

\[ f(x) = \beta_0\cdot\underbrace{1}_{b_0(x)} \;+\; \beta_1\cdot\underbrace{x}_{b_1(x)} \;+\; \beta_2\cdot\underbrace{(x-\kappa_1)^+}_{b_2(x)} \;+\; \beta_3\cdot\underbrace{(x-\kappa_2)^+}_{b_3(x)} \;+\; \cdots \;+\; \beta_{K+1}\cdot\underbrace{(x-\kappa_K)^+}_{b_{K+1}(x)} \]

The basis functions are:

\[ b_0(x) = 1, \qquad b_1(x) = x, \qquad b_{k+1}(x) = (x - \kappa_k)^+ \quad (k = 1, \dots, K). \]

So \(b_0\) is the constant, \(b_1\) is the plain straight line, and \(b_2, \dots, b_{K+1}\) are one hinge function for each knot. Then \(f(x)\) is the basis expansion \(E[Y \mid x] = \sum_{m=0}^{M}\beta_m b_m(x)\) with \(M = K+1\).

That is \(K + 2\) basis functions. This matches the parameter count from the setup: \(K+1\) lines with 2 parameters each gives \(2(K+1)\), and the \(K\) continuity constraints from Step 1 remove \(K\) of them, leaving \(2(K+1) - K = K + 2\).

The linear spline is therefore a linear model in \(\boldsymbol\beta\), even though the curve is not a straight line in \(x\). The expanded design matrix has one column per basis function:

\[ \underbrace{\Phi = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_1 & (x_1 - \kappa_1)^+ & \cdots & (x_1 - \kappa_K)^+ \\ 1 & x_2 & (x_2 - \kappa_1)^+ & \cdots & (x_2 - \kappa_K)^+ \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 1 & x_n & (x_n - \kappa_1)^+ & \cdots & (x_n - \kappa_K)^+ \end{bmatrix}}_{\Phi\ (n \times (K+2))} \]

so \(\Phi_{i,m} = b_m(x_i)\): column 0 is \(b_0\), column 1 is \(b_1\), and column \(k+1\) is \(b_{k+1}\). The model and the least squares estimate keep the same form as before:

\[ Y = \Phi\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon, \qquad \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\Phi'\Phi)^{-1}\Phi'Y . \]

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} Y_1 \\ Y_2 \\ \vdots \\ Y_n \end{bmatrix}}_{Y\ (n \times 1)} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_1 & (x_1 - \kappa_1)^+ & \cdots & (x_1 - \kappa_K)^+ \\ 1 & x_2 & (x_2 - \kappa_1)^+ & \cdots & (x_2 - \kappa_K)^+ \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 1 & x_n & (x_n - \kappa_1)^+ & \cdots & (x_n - \kappa_K)^+ \end{bmatrix}}_{\Phi\ (n \times (K+2))} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \beta_0 \\ \beta_1 \\ \beta_2 \\ \vdots \\ \beta_{K+1} \end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol\beta\ ((K+2) \times 1)} + \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \varepsilon_1 \\ \varepsilon_2 \\ \vdots \\ \varepsilon_n \end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol\varepsilon\ (n \times 1)} \]

6.4.7 Step 6: What the coefficients mean:

The slope of \(f_j(x)\) on interval \(I_j\) is the sum of the starting slope and all the slope changes passed so far:

\[ \gamma_j = \beta_1 + \sum_{k=1}^{j-1} \beta_{k+1}, \qquad x \in I_j . \]

  • \(\beta_0\) is the intercept of the first line.
  • \(\beta_1\) is the slope on the first interval \(I_1\).
  • \(\beta_{k+1}\) is the change in slope at knot \(\kappa_k\): the slope just left of \(\kappa_k\) is \(\gamma_k\), and just right of it is \(\gamma_k + \beta_{k+1} = \gamma_{k+1}\).

6.5 Cubic Splines

NoteDefinition

A cubic spline is the spline of degree \(d = 3\): a function made of cubic polynomial pieces that are joined at the knots. On each interval the curve is a cubic polynomial, and at each knot the two neighboring pieces must match in three ways:

  • the same value at each knot (\(f\) is continuous),
  • the same slope change at each knot (\(f'\) is continuous),
  • the same curvature at each knot (\(f''\) is continuous).

Because the slope is continuous, the curve has no kinks. It bends smoothly through the knots.

For child growth, this lets the curve bend gradually from fast early growth to slower later growth, without jumps and without sharp corners.

6.5.1 Mathematical Derivation:

Let \(\kappa_1, \dots, \kappa_K\) be the \(K\) knots, with endpoints \(\kappa_0 = a\) and \(\kappa_{K+1} = b\). The knots cut the \(x\)-axis into \(K+1\) intervals

\[ I_j = (\kappa_{j-1},\ \kappa_j], \qquad j = 1, \dots, K+1 . \]

The whole spline is \(f(x)\). On each interval it follows its own cubic polynomial \(f_j(x)\):

\[ f_j(x) = \alpha_j + \gamma_j\, x + \eta_j\, x^2 + \zeta_j\, x^3, \qquad f(x) = f_j(x) \ \text{ for } x \in I_j , \]

where \(\alpha_j\) is the intercept, \(\gamma_j\) is the coefficient of \(x\), \(\eta_j\) is the coefficient of \(x^2\), and \(\zeta_j\) is the coefficient of \(x^3\) for the cubic used on interval \(I_j\). Each \(f_j(x)\) is a full cubic curve that could be drawn for any \(x\), but it equals the spline \(f(x)\) only on \(I_j\).

The first and second derivatives of each piece are

\[ f_j'(x) = \gamma_j + 2\eta_j\, x + 3\zeta_j\, x^2, \qquad f_j''(x) = 2\eta_j + 6\zeta_j\, x . \]

6.5.2 Step 1: Impose continuity of the value, slope, and curvature at the knots:

For \(d = 3\), condition 2 of the definition requires that \(f(x)\), \(f'(x)\), and \(f''(x)\) all be continuous at every knot. At knot \(\kappa_j\), the cubic from the left side, \(f_j\), and the cubic from the right side, \(f_{j+1}\), must match in three ways at \(\kappa_j\):

\[ \begin{aligned} f_j(\kappa_j) &= f_{j+1}(\kappa_j) &&\text{(same height)}\\ f_j'(\kappa_j) &= f_{j+1}'(\kappa_j) &&\text{(same slope)}\\ f_j''(\kappa_j) &= f_{j+1}''(\kappa_j) &&\text{(same curvature)} \end{aligned} \qquad j = 1, \dots, K . \]

Written out with the coefficients, these are

\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha_j + \gamma_j\kappa_j + \eta_j\kappa_j^2 + \zeta_j\kappa_j^3 &= \alpha_{j+1} + \gamma_{j+1}\kappa_j + \eta_{j+1}\kappa_j^2 + \zeta_{j+1}\kappa_j^3, \\ \gamma_j + 2\eta_j\kappa_j + 3\zeta_j\kappa_j^2 &= \gamma_{j+1} + 2\eta_{j+1}\kappa_j + 3\zeta_{j+1}\kappa_j^2, \\ 2\eta_j + 6\zeta_j\kappa_j &= 2\eta_{j+1} + 6\zeta_{j+1}\kappa_j . \end{aligned} \]

6.5.3 Step 2: Determine how the cubic pieces differ between segments:

At a knot, two cubic pieces meet. Step 1 told us they must match in height, slope, and curvature. In Step 2, we ask what is left that can differ between them. To find out, take the difference between the right piece and the left piece:

\[ \delta_j(x) = f_{j+1}(x) - f_j(x) = (\alpha_{j+1} - \alpha_j) + (\gamma_{j+1} - \gamma_j)\,x + (\eta_{j+1} - \eta_j)\,x^2 + (\zeta_{j+1} - \zeta_j)\,x^3 . \]

\(\delta_j(x)\) is itself a cubic polynomial. The three matching rules from Step 1 say that this difference, and its first two derivatives, are all zero at the knot:

\[ \delta_j(\kappa_j) = 0, \qquad \delta_j'(\kappa_j) = 0, \qquad \delta_j''(\kappa_j) = 0 . \]

Any cubic can be re-written in powers of \((x - \kappa_j)\), that is, re-centered at the knot:

\[ \delta_j(x) = c_0 + c_1\,(x - \kappa_j) + c_2\,(x - \kappa_j)^2 + c_3\,(x - \kappa_j)^3 , \]

where \(c_0, c_1, c_2, c_3\) are numbers. Evaluate \(\delta_j\) and its derivatives at \(x = \kappa_j\). Every term with a factor of \((x - \kappa_j)\) vanishes there, so

\[ \delta_j(\kappa_j) = c_0, \qquad \delta_j'(\kappa_j) = c_1, \qquad \delta_j''(\kappa_j) = 2c_2 . \]

The three matching rules therefore force \(c_0 = c_1 = c_2 = 0\), and only the cubic term can survive:

\[ \delta_j(x) = c_3\,(x - \kappa_j)^3 . \]

The number \(c_3\) is the change in the \(x^3\) coefficient, \(\zeta_{j+1} - \zeta_j\), because re-centering does not change the leading coefficient. Give it a name:

\[ \beta_{j+3} = \zeta_{j+1} - \zeta_j . \]

Therefore each cubic is the previous cubic plus a cubed term that starts at the knot:

\[ \boxed{\,f_{j+1}(x) = f_j(x) + \beta_{j+3}(x - \kappa_j)^3\,} \]

Compare with the linear spline: there the new line was the old line plus a slope change times \((x - \kappa_j)\). Here the new cubic is the old cubic plus a change in the cubic coefficient times \((x - \kappa_j)^3\). The extra smoothness constraints are what remove the lower-power terms \((x-\kappa_j)^0\), \((x-\kappa_j)^1\), and \((x-\kappa_j)^2\).

6.5.4 Step 3: Applying Step 2 for all knot values (for each interval):

Start with the first interval and name its coefficients \(\beta_0 = \alpha_1\), \(\beta_1 = \gamma_1\), \(\beta_2 = \eta_1\), and \(\beta_3 = \zeta_1\), so that

\[ f_1(x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \beta_3 x^3 . \]

Applying Step 2 repeatedly:

\[ \begin{aligned} f_2(x) &= f_1(x) + \beta_4(x - \kappa_1)^3 = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \beta_3 x^3 + \beta_4(x - \kappa_1)^3, \\ f_3(x) &= f_2(x) + \beta_5(x - \kappa_2)^3 = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \beta_3 x^3 + \beta_4(x - \kappa_1)^3 + \beta_5(x - \kappa_2)^3, \\ &\ \ \vdots \\ f_j(x) &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \beta_3 x^3 + \sum_{k=1}^{j-1} \beta_{k+3}\,(x - \kappa_k)^3 . \end{aligned} \]

Since \(f(x) = f_j(x)\) for \(x \in I_j\), we have

\[ f(x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \beta_3 x^3 + \sum_{k=1}^{j-1} \beta_{k+3}\,(x - \kappa_k)^3, \qquad x \in I_j . \]

6.5.5 Step 4: Write one formula for all \(x\):

The formula in Step 3 includes a different number of terms on each interval. As in the linear spline, we write one formula by using the positive part \((u)^+ = \max(0,\, u)\).

Take \(x \in I_j = (\kappa_{j-1}, \kappa_j]\) and check that \(\left[(x - \kappa_k)^+\right]^3\) switches each term on or off correctly:

  • For knots already passed (\(k \le j-1\)): \(x > \kappa_{j-1} \ge \kappa_k\), so \(\left[(x - \kappa_k)^+\right]^3 = (x - \kappa_k)^3\). The term is on, as in Step 3.
  • For knots not yet reached (\(k \ge j\)): \(x \le \kappa_j \le \kappa_k\), so \(\left[(x - \kappa_k)^+\right]^3 = 0\). The term is off.

Because \(f(x) = f_j(x)\) on \(I_j\), a single formula for \(f(x)\) is correct on every interval at once:

\[ \boxed{\,f(x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \beta_3 x^3 + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \beta_{k+3}\,\left[(x - \kappa_k)^+\right]^3\,} \]

The model for the data is therefore

\[ E[Y \mid x] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \beta_3 x^3 + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \beta_{k+3}\,\left[(x - \kappa_k)^+\right]^3, \qquad \text{Var}(Y \mid x) = \sigma^2 . \]

6.5.6 Step 5: Read off the basis functions:

Label each term in the boxed formula from Step 4 by the basis function that multiplies its coefficient:

\[ f(x) = \beta_0\cdot\underbrace{1}_{b_0(x)} + \beta_1\cdot\underbrace{x}_{b_1(x)} + \beta_2\cdot\underbrace{x^2}_{b_2(x)} + \beta_3\cdot\underbrace{x^3}_{b_3(x)} + \beta_4\cdot\underbrace{\left[(x-\kappa_1)^+\right]^3}_{b_4(x)} + \cdots + \beta_{K+3}\cdot\underbrace{\left[(x-\kappa_K)^+\right]^3}_{b_{K+3}(x)} \]

The basis functions are:

\[ b_0(x) = 1, \quad b_1(x) = x, \quad b_2(x) = x^2, \quad b_3(x) = x^3, \quad b_{k+3}(x) = \left[(x - \kappa_k)^+\right]^3 \quad (k = 1, \dots, K). \]

So \(b_0\) through \(b_3\) build an ordinary cubic polynomial, and \(b_4, \dots, b_{K+3}\) are one cubed hinge function for each knot. Then \(f(x)\) is the basis expansion \(E[Y \mid x] = \sum_{m=0}^{M}\beta_m b_m(x)\) with \(M = K+3\).

That is \(K + 4\) basis functions. This matches the parameter count: \(K+1\) cubics with 4 parameters each gives \(4(K+1)\), and the \(3K\) constraints from Step 1 (three per knot) remove \(3K\) of them, leaving \(4(K+1) - 3K = K + 4\). This is the \(K + d + 1\) count from the earlier figure with \(d = 3\).

The cubic spline is therefore a linear model in \(\boldsymbol\beta\), even though the curve is not a straight line in \(x\). The expanded design matrix has one column per basis function:

\[ \underbrace{\Phi = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_1 & x_1^2 & x_1^3 & \left[(x_1 - \kappa_1)^+\right]^3 & \cdots & \left[(x_1 - \kappa_K)^+\right]^3 \\ 1 & x_2 & x_2^2 & x_2^3 & \left[(x_2 - \kappa_1)^+\right]^3 & \cdots & \left[(x_2 - \kappa_K)^+\right]^3 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 1 & x_n & x_n^2 & x_n^3 & \left[(x_n - \kappa_1)^+\right]^3 & \cdots & \left[(x_n - \kappa_K)^+\right]^3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\Phi\ (n \times (K+4))} \]

so \(\Phi_{i,m} = b_m(x_i)\): columns 0 to 3 are \(b_0\) to \(b_3\), and column \(k+3\) is \(b_{k+3}\). The model and the least squares estimate keep the same form as before:

\[ Y = \Phi\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon, \qquad \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\Phi'\Phi)^{-1}\Phi'Y . \]

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} Y_1 \\ Y_2 \\ \vdots \\ Y_n \end{bmatrix}}_{Y\ (n \times 1)} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_1 & x_1^2 & x_1^3 & \left[(x_1 - \kappa_1)^+\right]^3 & \cdots & \left[(x_1 - \kappa_K)^+\right]^3 \\ 1 & x_2 & x_2^2 & x_2^3 & \left[(x_2 - \kappa_1)^+\right]^3 & \cdots & \left[(x_2 - \kappa_K)^+\right]^3 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 1 & x_n & x_n^2 & x_n^3 & \left[(x_n - \kappa_1)^+\right]^3 & \cdots & \left[(x_n - \kappa_K)^+\right]^3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\Phi\ (n \times (K+4))} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \beta_0 \\ \beta_1 \\ \beta_2 \\ \beta_3 \\ \beta_4 \\ \vdots \\ \beta_{K+3} \end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol\beta\ ((K+4) \times 1)} + \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \varepsilon_1 \\ \varepsilon_2 \\ \vdots \\ \varepsilon_n \end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol\varepsilon\ (n \times 1)} \]

6.5.7 Step 6: What the coefficients mean:

The cubic used on interval \(I_j\) starts from the first cubic and picks up one change for each knot passed so far:

\[ f_j(x) = \underbrace{\beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2}_{\text{same as } f_1} + \underbrace{\Big(\beta_3 + \sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\beta_{k+3}\Big)}_{\zeta_j}\, x^3 + \cdots \]

and in particular the \(x^3\) coefficient of piece \(j\) is \(\zeta_j = \beta_3 + \sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\beta_{k+3}\).

  • \(\beta_0, \beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3\) are the coefficients of the cubic on the first interval \(I_1\).
  • \(\beta_{k+3}\) is the change in the cubic coefficient at knot \(\kappa_k\): the coefficient of \(x^3\) just left of \(\kappa_k\) is \(\zeta_k\), and just right of it is \(\zeta_k + \beta_{k+3} = \zeta_{k+1}\).
