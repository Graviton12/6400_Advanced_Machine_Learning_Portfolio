12  The Importance & Effects of Categorical Predictors in a Regression Model

12.1 Research Question:

The aim of this study is to examine two key questions:

  1. Variable selection: Can a method correctly identify which categorical predictors matter, in other words, which categorical variables are kept in the model?
  2. Category fusion: Can a method correctly identify categories that have identical effects, in other words, which levels of a categorical variable belong together?

12.2 Methodology:

To answer these questions, we will be conducting a simulation study that compares three different regularization methods:

  1. Ordinary Lasso regression
  2. one fusion-encouraging method from Tutz & Gertheiss (2016)
  3. a third method of our own design, or another one from Tutz & Gertheiss (2016)

12.2.1 Simulation Study Steps:

Data Generation Model

  1. Generate a simulated dataset where we know the truth using a data generation model.

Model Estimation

  1. Fit each regularization method to it using methods of our choice and methods highlighted in the Tutz & Gertheiss (2016) paper.
  2. Conduct parameterization on the categorical variables.
  3. Conduct paramter tuning to tune the values of parameters for a given method.

Result Evaluation

  1. Evaluate each fit against the truth.
  2. Repeat the simulation 500 times with fresh simulated data.
  3. Highlight the difference between predictive performance, variable-selection performance, and recovery of the underlying category structure
  4. Create visualizations to visually compare the performance of each method.

12.2.2 Regularization:

Regularization adds a oenalty term or constraint to the objective function for model estimation in order to stabilize estimation, reduce model size, protect against overfitting, or to incorporate additional information that exists independent of the data. Different regularization methods include:

Method Penalty or constraint Exact zeros? Point of action Tuning parameter(s) Role for categorical predictors
Lasso \(\lambda\lVert\beta\rVert_1\) Yes Individual \(\beta_j\) (L1) \(\lambda\) Selects individual dummy columns. Sets \(\beta_j\) coefficients to be exactly zero but cannot merge them directly, and arbitrarily keeps some levels and not others of a given categorical variable.
Ridge \(\lambda\lVert\beta\rVert_2^2\) No Individual \(\beta_j\) (L2) \(\lambda\) Shrinks \(\beta_j\) coefficients of a given categorical variable towards zero (with no parameterization) but never fuses.
Elastic net \(\lambda\left[\alpha\lVert\beta\rVert_1 + (1-\alpha)\lVert\beta\rVert_2^2\right]\) Yes Blend of L1 and L2 \(\lambda\), \(\alpha\) Kepps or drops correlated columns (correlated levels of a given categorical variable) together (grouping effect). Therefore, will keep or drop a given categorical variable but never fuses.
Bayesian lasso Laplace (double-exponential) prior on \(\beta_j\). The posterior mode matches the lasso estimate. Not in the posterior mean (zeros require a threshold or interval rule) Individual \(\beta_j\) through the prior Prior scale \(\lambda\) Same structure as lasso, plus posterior uncertainty for each level.
Principal Component Regression (PCR) Constraint \(\dot{R}_{k+}^{\top}\beta = 0\) (keep \(k\) components) In PC space yes, in \(\beta\) space no Linear combinations of \(\beta\) \(k\) (number of components) Components mix levels and categorical variables together, so it neither selects predictors nor fuses levels in an interpretable way.
Group lasso \(\lambda\sum_g w_g\lVert\beta_g\rVert_2\), with one group \(g\) per categorical variable Yes, for whole groups Blocks of coefficients \(\lambda\) (and group weights \(w_g\)) Variable selection. A given categorical variable is in or out, but levels inside a selected categorical variable are not fused.
Fusion \(\lambda\sum_{j<k} w_{jk}\lvert\beta_j-\beta_k\rvert\), over all pairs or adjacent levels Yes, in the differences (not in \(\beta\) itself) Differences between level effects \(\lambda\) (and weights \(w_{jk}\)) Category fusion. Equal level effects become exactly equal, and a categorical variable drops out only if all its levels are fused together.

12.2.3 Parameterization:

A categorical predictor with \(K\) levels must be converted into columns of the design matrix before it can enter a regression model. The parameterization is the choice of how to do this, and it determines what each coefficient means.

For this study, the parameterization determines the spartsity of the model (how many coefficients are shrunk to or near zero, dropping the corresponding predictor from the model). It determines whether a penalty can select a predictor (variable selection) or merge levels (category fusion).

For each method, we must consider the parameterization of the categorical predictors:

Parameterization Columns per factor Intercept is… Coefficient \(\beta_k\) is… \(\beta_k = 0\) means… Notes
Reference cell \(K-1\) mean of the reference level, \(\mu_{\text{ref}}\) level \(k\) minus the reference, \(\mu_k - \mu_{\text{ref}}\) level \(k\) equals the reference Penalties shrink levels toward the reference, so results depend on which level is chosen as the reference.
Cell means \(K\) none (dropped) the mean of level \(k\), \(\mu_k\) level \(k\) has mean zero With an intercept or several factors the columns are collinear and not identifiable. A regularization method is used where its penalty term picks one solution.
Unweighted average \(K-1\) unweighted average of the level means, \(\frac{1}{K}\sum_k \mu_k\) level \(k\) minus that average level \(k\) equals the average of the level means Constraint: \(\sum_k \beta_k = 0\). The omitted level’s effect is \(-\sum_{k<K}\beta_k\), so it is not penalized directly.
Weighted average (weighted effect coding) \(K-1\) overall mean of the data (levels weighted by sample size \(n_k\)) level \(k\) minus the overall mean level \(k\) equals the overall mean Constraint: \(\sum_k n_k \beta_k = 0\). The coding depends on the observed level counts \(n_k\), so it changes from dataset to dataset.

NoteVocabulary Note
  • Regression coefficient or (\(\beta\)) coefficient: a number saying how much a predictor’s level shifts/affects the outcome.
  • Sparsity: many coefficients being exactly zero.
  • Shrinkage: coefficient estimates pulled toward zero to reduce overfitting.
  • Overfitting: a model fitting on noise in the training data rather than true datapoints, which makes it predict new data poorly.

12.3 Simulation 1 - Simulation Study Tutz & Gertheiss (2016)

12.3.1 Data Generation Model:

The data generation model will be a standard linear regression model with 3 categorical variables: \(X_1\), \(X_2\), and \(X_3\)

Categorical variable Number of Levels Levels
\(X_1\) 3 1 - 3
\(X_2\) 10 1 - 10
\(X_3\) 20 1 - 20

The true linear model is represented by:

\[ Y = 0.5\,I_{(X_2 \in \{1\})} + 1\,I_{(X_2 \in \{2,3\})} + 1.75\,I_{(X_2 \in \{4,5,6\})} + 2.75\,I_{(X_2 \in \{7,8,9,10\})} + \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim N(0,1) \tag{12.1}\]

# Imports
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

# Random data generator
rng = np.random.default_rng(1) # seed 1
n = 200 # observations

# Each row gets a randomly chosen level, where all levels equally likely
X1 = pd.Categorical(rng.integers(1, 4,  size=n), categories=list(range(1, 4))) # draws 200 independent random whole numbers. Each is a number from 1 to 4
X2 = pd.Categorical(rng.integers(1, 11, size=n), categories=list(range(1, 11))) # draws 200 independent random whole numbers. Each is a number from 1 to 10
X3 = pd.Categorical(rng.integers(1, 21, size=n), categories=list(range(1, 21))) # draws 200 independent random whole numbers. Each is a number from 1 to 20

# True average of Y for levels 1 - 10 of X2
# The true average for X2 for level 1 --> 0.5
# The true average for X2 for levels 2, 3 --> 1
# The true average for X2 for levels 4, 5, 6 --> 1.75
# The true average for X2 for levels 7, 8, 9, 10 --> 2.75
effect_by_level = np.array([0.5, 1, 1, 1.75, 1.75, 1.75, 2.75, 2.75, 2.75, 2.75])

# True average --> converts each row's level into a position number starting at 0 (ex. level 1 --> index 0) and looksup true average of that level from array using the Index
true_average = effect_by_level[X2.codes]
# Noise --> draw 200 indepdendent random numbers from a standard norm dist with loc=0 (mean=0) and scale=1 (standard dev=1)
noise = rng.normal(loc=0, scale=1, size=n)

# Y = true average for that row's level of X2 + noise
Y = true_average + noise

# Build dataframe
data = pd.DataFrame({"X1": X1, "X2": X2, "X3": X3, "Y": Y})

A single simulated dataset has \(\text{N} = 200\) observations so it hav 200 rows. Each row gets its own independent noise draw \(\epsilon \sim N(0,1)\)

This dataset will be simulated 500 times. So across 500 simulated datasets: * Observations (rows): 200 rows × 500 = 100,000 rows * Noise values (\(\epsilon\)): 100,000 (one per row)

NoteUnderstadning the Model

Each \(I_{(X_2 \in \{level\})}\) is an indicator variable equals 1 if the condition inside is true for that row and 0 otherwise.

Indicator Equals 1 when… Levels of \(X_2\) covered Equivalent in dummy variables (\(D_k = I(X_2 = k)\)) \(\beta\) Coefficient (true effect)
\(I_{(X_2 \in \{1\})}\) \(X_2\) is level 1 1 \(D_1\) 0.5
\(I_{(X_2 \in \{2,3\})}\) \(X_2\) is level 2 or 3 2, 3 \(D_2 + D_3\) 1
\(I_{(X_2 \in \{4,5,6\})}\) \(X_2\) is level 4, 5, or 6 4, 5, 6 \(D_4 + D_5 + D_6\) 1.75
\(I_{(X_2 \in \{7,8,9,10\})}\) \(X_2\) is level 7, 8, 9, or 10 7, 8, 9, 10 \(D_7 + D_8 + D_9 + D_{10}\) 2.75

\(X_1\) and \(X_3\) do not appear in the model, so their true effects are zero. Each row of the data has exactly one indicator equal to 1, because the four sets of levels do not overlap and together cover all 10 levels of \(X_2\).

\(\epsilon \sim N(0,1)\) represents the noise in the data where each row of a given simulated dataset has an independent noise value drawn from a standard normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 1.

  • Mean 0: means the noise has no systematic push. It’s positive about as often as it’s negative, so it doesn’t change a level’s true average. It only scatters individual rows around it.
  • Standard deviation 1: sets the typical size of the scatter. About 68% of draws land within ±1 and about 95% within ±2
